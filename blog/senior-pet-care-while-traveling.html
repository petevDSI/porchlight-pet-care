Caring for Senior Pets While You Travel: What Sitters Need to Know

By Porchlight Pet & Home Care · October 29, 2026 · 2 min read

Senior pets have earned their routines. They also tend to have changing health needs, and small problems can become big ones quickly. A thoughtful plan keeps them comfortable when you are away.

When is a pet "senior"?

It varies by species and size. Many dogs are considered seniors around age seven, with large breeds aging sooner. Cats are often considered seniors around age ten. Your vet can tell you where your pet is on the aging path.

Common age-related conditions

  • Arthritis and joint pain
  • Kidney disease and thyroid problems
  • Heart disease
  • Diabetes
  • Dental disease
  • Vision and hearing loss
  • Cognitive changes, sometimes called canine or feline cognitive dysfunction

Before you travel

  1. Schedule a senior wellness exam. Many vets recommend checkups twice a year for older pets, and bloodwork when appropriate.
  2. Review medications and supplements. Make sure you have enough, and write down timing.
  3. Discuss mobility. Ask if your pet needs ramps, non-slip rugs or help with stairs and car rides.
  4. Ask your vet whether your pet is fit for the trip you are planning and what the sitter should watch for.
  5. Talk about end-of-life questions if your pet is seriously ill, so your sitter knows what you want in a crisis.

Set up the home

  • Non-slip flooring or runners on slick surfaces
  • Orthopedic bedding on the main level
  • Water and food bowls at a comfortable height
  • Nightlights for pets with vision changes
  • Easy access to litter boxes with low sides for cats

What a sitter should know

  • Bathroom needs: Seniors may need more frequent outings, and may have accidents.
  • Walk pace: Shorter, slower walks, and rest breaks.
  • Appetite and water intake: Changes can signal problems.
  • Temperature sensitivity: Older pets are more vulnerable to heat and cold.
  • Sleep and routines: Stick to the same schedule.
  • Signs to report: New limping, coughing, confusion, accidents, extreme thirst, sudden weight loss, or reluctance to eat.

Choose care that fits

Seniors often do best at home, with a sitter who offers more frequent visits, or stays overnight. Boarding can be stressful for older animals. If you do use a facility, make sure they can handle medications and mobility needs.

Gentle comfort

Slow, calm interactions, soft bedding and a predictable schedule matter more to many seniors than excitement. Give your sitter permission to take things slow.

Share the story: Tell your sitter about your pet's history, favorite spots and habits. Older pets are often best understood through stories.

With a bit of extra planning, travel does not have to mean worrying about your oldest companion.
